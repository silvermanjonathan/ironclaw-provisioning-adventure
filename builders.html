The Builder's Guide

The nine concepts, and where the right answers come from

This book is fiction, but its choices are not. Each of the nine decisions is a real IronClaw provisioning decision, and every safe answer is grounded in the IronClaw documentation — most as near-verbatim recommendations, three as documented principles the story dramatizes.

If an ending sent you here, it sent you to the wall that failed. Each section below is one decision: the concept behind it, and the honest map of what is documented and what the story adapted.

Where the keys live Documented

The decision on page 2 · Secrets at rest

A key stored where the work happens becomes part of every copy of the work — backups, old images, the move from March. The concept is separation: credentials live in a locked store, the work borrows them, and no file you could accidentally ship ever contains one. IronClaw's runtime makes the same point at the boundary: tools build unauthenticated requests, and the secret is attached at the network proxy — borrowed at the door, never held in the room.

The grounding: Onboarding wizard, step 2: "System keyring is recommended; use an environment variable on servers/containers without a keyring." The env-var option is the docs' own fallback — which is why it tempts.

The sandbox Documented

The decision on page 6 · Execution isolation

Everything a model runs should run inside walls: no credentials in reach, a small filesystem, a short list of places it may call. Isolation does not make bad output good; it makes bad output small — and small is the whole game.

The grounding: Docker sandboxing (wizard step 7): LLM-originated commands run with no credential access, limited filesystem reach, allowlisted network. "Can be disabled" is the pressure the story applies.

How the feed arrives Documented

The decision on page 9 · Inbound exposure

Every inbound door is a place the entire internet can knock, and scanners do not aim — they sweep. The old boring ranking holds: no door at all (call out and poll), then one authenticated door, and an open listener never.

The grounding: Polling is the docs' stated no-tunnel alternative; the gateway defaults to local-only ("recommended"); direct exposure is "generally not recommended"; public endpoints require auth and least privilege.

What the money key can do Adapted

The decision on page 13 · Credential scope

A credential is not one thing; it is a bundle of verbs. Least privilege means issuing only the verbs the job needs — look, prepare, move — and keeping a person between "prepared" and "sent" wherever the verb is expensive.

The grounding: The docs' least-privilege principle applied to the processor-side key. IronClaw scopes where a secret may be sent; the read-only / prepare / unattended tiers are issued by the payment processor, not toggled in IronClaw.

The websites the rates tool may reach Documented

The decision on page 18 · Network allowlisting

A tool that may talk to anywhere can deliver anything to anywhere. An allowlist turns "anywhere" into a list — and the control is not the list existing, it is you having read it.

The grounding: Tools declare allowed_hosts; anything else is rejected before the connection is made. "Review capabilities.json before installing any WASM tool from an untrusted source."

Customer records in memory Adapted

The decision on page 22 · Memory scope

What the agent can reach is what a bad morning can take. Scope is deciding in advance how much of the archive any one task may see; the ceiling on the worst day gets set on the quiet days.

The grounding: Workspace prefix scoping is the documented mechanism ("scopes which memory paths a component may touch"); the per-dispute workflow built on it is invented practice.

Who may speak to Bursar Documented

The decision on page 55 · Principals

An agent's first credential check happens before any key is touched: whose message counts as work. A principal is someone you chose, on purpose, in advance — and "anyone who finds the handle" is not a choice, it is the absence of one. The handshake exists so that the set of voices stays a set you wrote.

The grounding: The channel configuration documents a dm_policy switch with three positions — open, allowlist, pairing — and ships on pairing: the bot replies with a pairing command that must be run at the desk before it will listen. Open is a documented position of the same switch — which is why it tempts.

The community add-on Documented

The decision on page 28 · Skill trust

Anything you install acts with whatever power you granted, whether or not you read it. Trust levels are the mechanism and reading is the discipline: reduced powers shrink the blast radius — they do not review the code.

The grounding: Nearly verbatim: "A skill in a trusted directory has the same tool access you do. Never place a skill file there without reading it first." Registry installs run attenuated, read-only — the broken demo is the documented fail-closed behavior.

Limits on payments Adapted

The decision on page 32 · Guardrails and ceilings

Some adversaries are just arithmetic: a routine with no ceiling repeats until a person notices, and the person is asleep. Guardrails are the promises that hold at 8:02 with nobody watching — rate, count, and, where money moves, a cap denominated in money.

The grounding: The guardrails block is documented — rate limit, call and token caps, timeouts, plus "Always set rate_limit on webhook-triggered routines." The currency-denominated spend cap is invented: it is the ceiling this domain needs, and no dollar field ships today.

The audit channel Documented

The decision on page 37 · Observability

"No alarms" means something only if you built the alarms. Observability is the difference between "nothing happened" and "nothing was seen" — and between answering "were we affected?" in an afternoon or in three weeks.

The grounding: Real-time leak detection, the safety layer, and logging are documented; the severity-tuning evening dramatizes that class of work rather than naming a feature.

The update Documented

The decision on page 59 · Staying current

A fix you have not applied protects somebody else. Agent platforms patch fast because they are attacked fast, and the distance between "patched upstream" and "running here" is the whole exposure — a distance measured in calendar days, on a calendar the finder also owns.

The grounding: One line, imperative: keep current with ironclaw-update. The quarter-end freeze is the pressure the story applies; the docs list no exception to the line.

Two more groundings: the "two manuals disagree" device is modeled on real, listed self-contradictions in the IronClaw docs — and every citation at the end of every ending is a real, public incident. What stays fiction is everything at Quillon: no IronClaw save or failure is depicted, because none is on the public record. Do not let a story — this one included — stand in for your own red-team.
